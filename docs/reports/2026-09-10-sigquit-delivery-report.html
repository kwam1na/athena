Executive summary

PR #839 teaches Athena's TypeScript delivery wrapper to handle POSIX SIGQUIT as a cooperative shutdown request. The wrapper forwards SIGQUIT to the installed launcher, keeps waiting for that child to finish its cleanup, and returns the conventional exit status 131.

The candidate also installs the exact qualified linear-concurrent-delivery-v1 artifact with runtime 0.5.0. That installed launcher owns process-group drainage and bounded escalation, while Athena owns signal translation and waiting. The two layers are tested together through the real installed path.

Why this mattered

Athena already forwarded SIGINT, SIGTERM, and SIGHUP. SIGQUIT was missing. A direct SIGQUIT could stop at the Bun wrapper, and a terminal-group SIGQUIT could end the wrapper before the launcher and worker had demonstrably exited.

That gap mattered because an exit code alone does not prove cleanup. The delivery process may own a worker group that needs a cooperative signal, a short drain window, and an uncatchable escalation only when a worker refuses to stop.

Mental model: translator, supervisor, workers

Think of Athena's wrapper as a translator at the boundary. It receives the operator-facing SIGQUIT, remembers that the final wrapper status must be 131, forwards the same signal, and waits.

The installed Python launcher is the supervisor. It signals the isolated worker process group, allows cooperative drainage, sends SIGKILL only after the bounded deadline when a worker resists, and waits for its direct child. The workers either cooperate with SIGQUIT or exercise that escalation path.

Before and after

Before: SIGINT, SIGTERM, and SIGHUP crossed the Bun boundary, while SIGQUIT could terminate or stop at the wrapper without a complete descendant-cleanup proof.

After: SIGQUIT crosses the Bun boundary on POSIX hosts, reaches the installed launcher and worker group, and the wrapper does not return until the launcher has completed. Direct delivery and terminal process-group delivery follow the same ownership model.

  • Cooperative workers record SIGQUIT and exit promptly.
  • Resistant workers record SIGQUIT, remain alive through the cooperative window, and are stopped by the launcher's bounded SIGKILL escalation.
  • The wrapper reports 131 only after launcher completion, while existing SIGINT, SIGTERM, and SIGHUP behavior remains covered.

Mechanics across the installed path

scripts/delivery-product.ts routes SIGQUIT registration and removal through a small platform-aware listener helper. The ordinary command path supplies the real process callbacks. Its first observed interruption status wins, so duplicate terminal-group delivery does not rewrite the result. The listener is removed after child completion.

The test fixture writes launcher and worker PIDs and records the actual signal seen by the worker. After the wrapper exits, each case checks the recorded SIGQUIT and verifies that both descendant processes are absent or zombies. This makes the absence of live descendants, rather than exit status alone, part of the asserted outcome.

The installed bytes came from the qualified archive with SHA-256 e63b1f2bb5516d55445aa7050f2a9366b05abdb9d8584bdeed4119abb5440578 and metadata SHA-256 d46f7f75e11b7f809f93e10b961954bfd7f8dc533f474074aa57b5594d39868e. Athena did not hand-edit the generated generation.

Failure boundaries

SIGQUIT support is POSIX-specific. A simulated win32 listener-lifecycle row proves Windows performs neither SIGQUIT registration nor removal, while a POSIX row proves both operations. A separate ordinary-command row controls the real process platform input and observes the actual process callbacks, which binds the helper guard to the production wrapper path. The embedded capture override remains on its existing AbortController and SIGINT path.

SIGKILL cannot be caught or cooperatively forwarded. It remains an installed-launcher escalation after the cooperative deadline, not a new Athena wrapper signal.

A terminal can deliver SIGQUIT to both wrapper and launcher. The implementation tolerates repeated delivery by preserving the first interruption number and allowing already-drained process groups to disappear safely. Future ownership changes must rerun both direct and terminal-group cases.

The regression cases send SIGQUIT only after the launcher and worker PID files exist. They exercise steady-state shutdown and do not cover SIGQUIT arriving during launcher startup.

  • Exercise both direct signal delivery and terminal process-group delivery.

  • Install a qualified archive with its matching release metadata and --maintenance; never patch the tracked generation by hand.
